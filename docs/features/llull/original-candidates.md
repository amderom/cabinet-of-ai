# The original — three candidate passages (Task 10)

> 2026-11-13. All three from Ramon Llull, *Ars brevis* (Pisa, January 1308), in *Raymundi Lullii opera ea quae ad adinventam ab ipso artem universalem … pertinent*, Strasbourg, heirs of Lazarus Zetzner, 1617. Copy of the Bibliothèque interuniversitaire de santé, Paris, scanned on the Internet Archive ([BIUSante_pharma_res011289](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_pharma_res011289)); licence: Licence Ouverte / Etalab (credit "BIU Santé (Paris)"). Latin transcribed by the agent from the page images (long s as s, ligatures expanded, 1617 spelling otherwise kept); English drafts by the agent, to translate together with the author.

## A. The opening: "to answer all questions" (p. 1, leaf n25)

> Deus, cum tua gratia, sapientia, & amore. Incipit ars brevis, quae est imago artis generalis, quae sic intitulatur: Deus, cum tua summa perfectione, incipit ars generalis ultima.
> Ratio quare facimus istam artem brevem est, ut ars magna facilius sciatur. Nam scita ista arte, supra dicta, & etiam aliae artes de facili possunt sciri & addisci. Subjectum huius artis est respondere de omnibus quaestionibus, supposito quod sciatur quid dicitur per nomen.

**Draft:** God, with your grace, wisdom and love: here begins the Short Art, which is the image of the general Art entitled "God, with your supreme perfection: here begins the Ultimate General Art". We make this short Art so that the great Art may be known more easily. For once this one is known, the Art above, and other arts too, can easily be known and learned. The subject of this Art is to answer all questions, provided one knows what is meant by the name.

## B. The fourth figure: the wheels (p. 9, leaf n33)

> Quarta figura habet tres circulos, quorum superior est immobilis: duo autem inferiores sunt mobiles, ut in figura patet. Circulus medius volvitur sub circulo superiori immobili: ut puta quando ponitur c. sub b. circulus autem inferior volvitur sub circulo medio, sicut quando ponitur d. sub c. & tunc temporis formantur novem camerae, b.c.d. est una camera, c.d.e. est alia, & sic de aliis deinceps. […] & sic per media camerarum homo venatur necessarias conclusiones & invenit illas.

**Draft:** The fourth figure has three circles: the upper one does not move, but the two below it do, as the figure shows. The middle circle turns under the fixed upper circle, as when C is set under B; and the lower circle turns under the middle one, as when D is set under C. At that moment nine chambers are formed: BCD is one chamber, CDE another, and so on in turn. […] And so, through the middle terms of the chambers, a man hunts down necessary conclusions and finds them.

## C. The end: three friends, and the date (p. 42, leaf n66)

> Unde sciendum est quod haec ars tres amicos habet, videlicet subtilitatem intellectus, & rationem, & bonam intentionem: sine istis quidem tribus hanc artem nullus addiscere potest. […]
> Ad honorem & laudem Dei & publicae utilitatis finivit Raymundus hunc librum, Pisis in monasterio Sancti Dominici in mense Ianuarii, in anno Domini M.CCCVII. incarnationis Domini nostri Iesu Christi, cui sit laus & honos per infinita secula seculorum. Amen.

**Draft:** Know, then, that this Art has three friends: subtlety of intellect, reason, and good intention. Without these three, no one can learn it. […] To the honour and praise of God, and for the public good, Raymond finished this book at Pisa, in the monastery of Saint Dominic, in the month of January, in the year 1307 of the Incarnation of our Lord Jesus Christ (January 1308 by our reckoning), to whom be praise and honour for ever and ever. Amen.

## D. The pitch to the Pope: Petition to Celestine V (Naples, 1294)

> Source: *Petitio Raymundi pro conversione infidelium ad Coelestinum V*, Latin text printed in full by Girolamo Golubovich, *Biblioteca bio-bibliografica della Terra Santa e dell'Oriente francescano*, vol. I (Quaracchi, 1906), pp. 373–375; University of Toronto copy on the Internet Archive ([bibliotecabiobib01colluoft](https://archive.org/details/bibliotecabiobib01colluoft), "not in copyright"). Peers (1929, pp. 252–254) paraphrases it. Latin below from the OCR, cleaned (u/n confusions): **to check word by word against the page images before use.** Excerpts: the opening, Llull's offer, the ending.

> Cum Deus principaliter creaverit hominem, ut homo ipsum recolat, intelligat, amet, honoret, et ipsi serviat, et cum sint tot infideles euntes ad ignem perdurabilem […] et etiam cum sint tot, quod credo, quod pro uno Christiano sint centum vel plures qui non sunt Christiani, multum esset conveniens, quod vos, sancte Episcope Coelestine Quinte, […] et Domini honorati et discreti Cardinales aperiretis thesaurum sanctae Ecclesiae […]
> Ego Raymundus Lullus indignus aestimo me multas tales [rationes] habere secundum aliquem novum modum, quem Deus mihi dedit ad vincendum omnes illos, qui contra Fidem Catholicam aliquid volunt probare vel improbare. […]
> Multas alias rationes possem dicere, sed timeo nimium loqui, et si nimium loquor, supplico et peto veniam […] ut vobis placeat me indignum primum mittere ad Saracenos ad honorandum inter illos nostrum Dominum Deum. Data est haec petitio in civitate Neapolitana sancto Patri Coelestino Quinto, et honoratis Dominis Cardinalibus, anno MCCXCIV.

**Draft:** Since God created man above all so that man might remember Him, understand, love, honour and serve Him, and since so many unbelievers are going to everlasting fire […], so many that, I believe, for every Christian there are a hundred or more who are not, it would be most fitting that you, holy Bishop Celestine the Fifth, […] and you, honoured and discreet lord Cardinals, should open the treasury of Holy Church. […] I, Ramon Llull, unworthy, believe I have many such reasons, by a certain new method that God gave me, to overcome all who would prove or disprove anything against the Catholic faith. […] I could give many other reasons, but I fear to say too much; and if I say too much, I beg your pardon […], and that it may please you to send me, unworthy as I am, first to the Saracens, to honour our Lord God among them. This petition was given in the city of Naples to the Holy Father Celestine the Fifth and the honoured lord Cardinals, in the year 1294.

## E. How to teach the Art (Ars brevis, ch. XLVI, p. 42, leaf n66), with the closing note

> Pars ista dividitur in quatuor partes. Prima est ut artista bene sciat alphabetum cordetenus, & figuras, diffinitiones, & regulas atque assituationem tabulae. Secunda pars est ut ipse declaret bene textum scolaribus rationabiliter, & non liget se cum auctoritatibus aliorum. Et quod scolares perlegant textum, & si dubitaverint in aliquo petant ab artista sive a magistro illud. Tertia pars est quod magister sive artista faciat quaestiones coram scolaribus & solvat eas rationabiliter secundum artis processum. Nam absque ratione artista non potest bene uti ista arte. Unde sciendum est quod haec ars tres amicos habet, videlicet subtilitatem intellectus, & rationem, & bonam intentionem: sine istis quidem tribus hanc artem nullus addiscere potest. Quarta pars est quod artista faciat scolaribus quaestiones ut ipsi de illis respondeant: & dicat illis quod multiplicent rationes ad unam & eandem conclusionem, & adhuc inveniant loca quorum ratione sciant respondere & rationes multiplicare. Si vero scolares nesciant respondere, nec rationes multiplicare, nec loca invenire, tunc temporis magister doceat scolares de praedictis.

**Draft:** This part is divided into four. First, the artist must know the alphabet by heart, and the figures, the definitions, the rules and the layout of the table. Second, he must explain the text to the students by reason, and not tie himself to other people's authorities; and the students must read the text through, and if they are in doubt about anything, ask the artist, that is, the master. Third, the master or artist must set questions in front of the students and solve them by reason, following the Art's procedure. For without reason the artist cannot use this Art well. Know, then, that this Art has three friends: subtlety of intellect, reason, and good intention. Without these three, no one can learn it. Fourth, the artist must set the students questions for them to answer, and tell them to multiply reasons towards one and the same conclusion, and to find the places that let them answer and multiply reasons. And if the students cannot answer, nor multiply reasons, nor find the places, then the master must teach them all of the above. (Followed by the closing note, C.)
