Llull wrote that his Art had three friends: subtlety of intellect, reason, and good intention. For this second exhibit, I had a fourth: the same AI coding agent that rebuilt [the Lagado Engine](/exhibits/lagado/making-of/), working with the same method and the same rules. It took one day, 13 November 2026.

The machine was new, though, and so were the difficulties. Here are three of them, told once more as conversations which never quite happened in these words.

<Scene label="1 · The quotes">
  <Fragment slot="title">It <em>paraphrased.</em> I wanted the exact words.</Fragment>
  <Line who="me">Where does this answer come from?</Line>
  <Line who="agent">Llull, Ars generalis ultima.</Line>
  <Line who="me">Which part? Whose translation?</Line>
  <Line who="agent">Part 11. My own reading of it.</Line>
  <Line who="me">Then it isn’t Llull. Quote him word for word, and credit the translator.</Line>
  <Line who="agent">Done. Peers, page 321.</Line>
  <Line who="me">Page 322. Check again.</Line>
  <Point slot="point">A quotation is a small contract: these exact words, by this person, found in this place, in this translation. The agent wrote fluently and attributed generously, which, as Descartes said of Llull’s Art, is a way of speaking without judgment of what one does not know. So the rule became a habit: every line given to Llull is his, in Yanis Dambergs’ translation, credited, and checked by a script against the source; every page number is opened, not remembered; and when the agent reads Llull its own way, the page says so.</Point>
</Scene>

<Scene label="2 · The research">
  <Fragment slot="title">Swift I knew. Llull, <em>I had to ask.</em></Fragment>
  <Line who="me">I know Gulliver inside out. Llull, much less. How does the Art really work?</Line>
  <Line who="agent">Three ideas for the machine: a volvelle, a funnel, a chatbot.</Line>
  <Line who="me">None of them is faithful to Llull.</Line>
  <Line who="agent">Reading the Ars generalis ultima… Eighty-four columns of twenty chambers. Only one question goes through them.</Line>
  <Line who="me">Why only one?</Line>
  <Line who="agent">The others point to a paragraph elsewhere in the book. And here is a pitch he wrote to a pope, printed in 1906.</Line>
  <Point slot="point">With Swift, I was the specialist and the agent the assistant. With Llull, the roles moved: the agent read a Lyon edition of 1517, a Strasbourg Latin printing of 1617, an Italian bibliography of 1906 and a biography of 1929, and came back with page numbers and a translation of the Latin. My part became asking the questions a reader would ask (why only one? did the Pope really say yes?) and checking that the answers held. Outsourcing research does not outsource doubt.</Point>
</Scene>

<Scene label="3 · The second time">
  <Fragment slot="title">The second exhibit was <em>easier.</em> That was the point.</Fragment>
  <Line who="me">Same plates as Lagado?</Line>
  <Line who="agent">Shared between the two. Lagado: zero pixels changed.</Line>
  <Line who="me">And the credits, page by page, for both.</Line>
  <Line who="agent">Done, and written down for the next exhibit.</Line>
  <Point slot="point">A second exhibit is where a method proves itself. The rules learnt on Lagado (vintage is content, never look; ask before changing; check everything on a phone) were already written down, so the agent followed them instead of discovering them again: seven prototypes instead of twenty-eight. What stayed hard was not the code but the content, which is to say, what Llull actually said.</Point>
</Scene>
