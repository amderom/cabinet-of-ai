In Book III of *Gulliver’s Travels* (1726), the professors of the Academy of Lagado build a machine to write everything ever worth writing — “a complete body of all arts and sciences.” The method? A wooden frame strung with word-covered dice, forty handles, and thirty-six students dutifully scribbling down any phrase that doesn’t sound completely deranged. It is, of course, satire aimed at the Royal Society. It is also, awkwardly, a startup pitch three centuries early. Ramon Llull had cranked the same dream in 1305 with rotating paper wheels; the Lagado professor just added the funding request — he wants five hundred more frames, if only the public would kindly raise the capital.

## Monkey Business and Mathematical Fuss

Does the thing work? Alarmingly, almost. Run the numbers and the fictional engine could spit out more texts than there are atoms to be smug about — figures trailing hundreds of thousands of digits. Swift stumbles here onto a paradox the twentieth century would formalise: Borel’s monkey, hammering a keyboard until it accidentally types *Hamlet*, and Borges’ *Library of Babel*, shelving every book that could ever be. Both reveal the catch our professor cheerfully ignores — you can reach the infinite from the finite, technically, given rather more time than the universe has budgeted.

## Neurons and Neurotics

Swift’s professor “made the strictest computation of the general proportion there is in books between the numbers of particles, nouns, and verbs” — which is, disconcertingly, the founding principle of natural language processing: teach a machine the odds that one word follows another, and let it generate accordingly. When this article was written, those generators were charmingly hopeless. Feed *Gulliver’s Travels* into one and it coughed up phrases so odd they’d make a Surrealist weep with pride — bidets solemnly declared machines, “the other is the same,” accidental Rimbaud. The eighteenth-century satire had apparently been doing automatic writing before the avant-garde thought to rebel.

## Same Old, Same Old

Yet the dream quietly deflates — though not quite where my original paper first spotted the leak. In 2020 the convergent, single-“best”-answer machine looked like the translation engine: DeepL, Google Translate and their kin, weighing each word against its likeliest neighbours to settle, primly, on one rendering. Generation was then the charming wild card. Six years on, the roles have swapped — generation is the triumph, and it turns out to totalize too. Trained to produce the likeliest, most agreeable sentence, the large language model doesn’t fling open the infinite readings a text contains so much as slump toward a fluent median: the faintly interchangeable prose we’ve all learned to spot at a glance. The machine stopped promising infinity and started delivering uniformity by another door. “Saying everything” still doesn’t mean more; it means one. All that glorious polysemy — the very thing that made a text literary — gets ironed flat.

## Web of Deceit

In *The Battle of the Books*, Swift set the industrious spider — spinning webs entirely out of its own guts — against the bee that gathers from the wide world. The moderns, he sneered, are spiders. And so is the machine: it produces only what its innards already hold. Fed the past, GPT-3 faithfully regurgitated its prejudices rather than thinking anything new. The encyclopedic fantasy — now rebranded as Silicon Valley’s shiny AI humanism — sets out for the infinity of knowledge and arrives, slightly winded, at its totalization: dirt spun into a cobweb the first breeze lifts away.
